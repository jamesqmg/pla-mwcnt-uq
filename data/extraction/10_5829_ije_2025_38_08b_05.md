## **1. INTRODUCTION** 

Polylactic acid (PLA) is a biodegradable polymer derived from renewable resources such as corn starch or sugarcane, making it an environmentally friendly alternative to traditional petroleum-based plastics (1). Its attractive properties, including biocompatibility, high strength-to-weight ratio, and ease of processing, have led to its widespread use in industries such as packaging, biomedical devices, and 3D printing (2, 3). However, despite its advantages, PLA suffers from certain limitations, particularly its brittleness, low thermal stability, and limited electrical conductivity. To overcome these shortcomings, researchers have explored the incorporation of nanomaterials, such as multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), into PLA matrices to enhance its mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties (4-6). 

MWCNTs, a class of carbon-based nanomaterials, possess extraordinary mechanical strength, high electrical conductivity, and excellent thermal stability (79). These unique properties make MWCNTs an ideal candidate for reinforcing polymer matrices, including PLA (10, 11). When uniformly dispersed in the PLA matrix, MWCNTs can form conductive networks, leading to enhanced electrical conductivity and improved mechanical properties. Additionally, MWCNTs have improved the thermal stability and crystallinity of PLA, making the composite more suitable for highperformance applications (12, 13). 

The combination of PLA and MWCNTs results in a composite material with a unique balance of biodegradability, mechanical strength, and electrical conductivity, making it an attractive option for a wide range of applications, including electronic devices, structural components, and advanced packaging. However, challenges remain in achieving homogeneous dispersion of MWCNTs within the PLA matrix, which is critical for optimizing the composite's properties. Research in this field continues to focus on improving dispersion techniques, tailoring the interfacial interactions between PLA and MWCNTs, and understanding the structure-property relationships that govern the performance of PLA/MWCNT composites. 

The fabrication of PLA/MWCNT composite filament for applications such as 3D printing or other manufacturing processes involves several key steps, focusing on the uniform dispersion of MWCNTs within the PLA matrix (14, 15). The manufacturing process typically involves the following methods: solution mixing method, melt blending method, in situ polymerization method, electrospinning, and etc. (1619). Once the PLA/MWCNT composite material has been prepared through one of the above methods, it is typically fed into an extruder to form filament. During extrusion, the material is heated and forced through a 

narrow die, shaping it into a filament of the desired diameter. The filament is then cooled, collected onto spools, and stored for later use in 3D printing or other applications. 

Despite the advantages of PLA/MWCNT composites, there are several challenges in the manufacturing process. Achieving uniform dispersion of MWCNTs within the PLA matrix is critical for optimizing the composite’s properties. Aggregation of MWCNTs can lead to weak points in the filament. Enhancing the interfacial bonding between hydrophobic MWCNTs and hydrophilic PLA is crucial for improved mechanical performance (17). The high viscosity of the PLA/MWCNT mixture during the melt processing can make extrusion more challenging, required optimization of the processing parameters. Various researchers have investigated the manufacturing of PLA-MWCNT composites. Ivanov et al. (20) explored PLA/MWCNT composites with varying filler content (06 wt.%) and observed that electrical and thermal conductivity significantly increased with addition of the filler content. A synergistic effect was noted in bi-filler PLA composites containing both graphene and MWCNTs. Lage-Rivera et al. (21) demonstrated the use of lignin as a bio-dispersant, which improved the electrical conductivity of PLA/MWCNT composites and enhanced printability. Electrical percolation was achieved at 5 wt.% MWCNT. Vidakis et al. (22) focused on the fabrication and characterization of PLA-MWCNT composite filaments using fused filament fabrication (FFF), a common 3D printing technology. This work demonstrates the multifunctional capabilities of PLAMWCNT composites, suggesting their suitability for various industrial applications, including electronics and healthcare. Silva et al. (23) explored how addition of a biodegradable plasticizer to PLA improved the dispersion of MWCNTs, leading to better flexibility and electrical performance. Selected composites were tested for sensing properties using acetone vapor, demonstrating a positive response suitable for wearable devices. Yohannan et al. (24) conducted a research on creating hybrid nanocomposite filaments using multiwalled carbon nanotubes and montmorillonite (MMT) as reinforcements for polylactic acid. Their study revealed that incorporating MWCNT and MMT at different weight percentages (0.5% to 2%) enhanced the tensile, thermal, and electrical properties of the PLA filaments. Stukhlyak  et al. (25) investigated the shock fracture mechanisms of epoxy composite materials reinforced with varying concentrations of silicon carbide microparticles. The findings reveal that the high heterogeneity of epoxy composites across structural scales significantly influences their physical and mechanical properties. Under dynamic loading, the material exhibits a hierarchical structural organization, enabling cohesive deformation and fracture, predominantly driven by rotational deformation modes.

Microcracks emerge due to low shear strain, further restricted by the reinforcing particles. While microscale mechanisms dominate the initiation of a primary crack, its propagation is governed by macroscale fracture mechanisms. 

Fused deposition modeling, a popular additive manufacturing technique, has seen growing applications in producing advanced composite materials due to its cost-effectiveness and ease of use (26). Among the composites being explored, PLA reinforced with MWCNTs has emerged as a material of significant interest. The Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) process for PLA-MWCNT composites involves extruding thermoplastic filaments that are loaded with dispersed MWCNTs (27). The success of this method hinges on achieving a uniform distribution of nanotubes in the polymer matrix, which can significantly impact the final properties of the printed part. Various studies have demonstrated the potential of these composites to improve tensile strength, thermal stability, and electrical conductivity, extending their utility in fields like electronics, biomedical devices, and automotive components. Research on PLA-MWCNT composites not only highlights the potential for enhanced material properties but also points to the challenges associated with their fabrication, including issues related to MWCNT dispersion, cost, and scalability of production (17, 28). Despite these challenges, the ongoing exploration of PLA-MWCNT composites in FDM technology shows promise for the future of 3D-printed functional materials. 

Research into PLA/MWCNT composites is essential for advancing the field of 3D printing and material science, particularly given the growing demand for materials with enhanced mechanical properties. The geometric configuration of support brackets—triangular, inclined, and curved—plays a crucial role in this context, as different shapes influence stress distribution and loadbearing capabilities. Understanding these mechanical responses is vital for optimizing the performance of printed structures. The study highlights that welldispersed MWCNTs enhance load transfer and improve overall strength, while agglomeration at higher concentrations leads to weak points within the matrix. By examining the interplay between CNT concentration, printing orientation, and geometric design, this research addresses critical gaps in knowledge and supports the development of more effective composite materials for a variety of applications . 

## **2. MATERIALS AND METHODS** 

Due to limited mechanical strength of PLA filament, a new PLA filament enhanced with carbon nanotubes was developed, with carbon nanotube weight percentages of 

1%, 3%, and 5%. Table 1 presents the properties of the PLA filament. The MWCNTs, with a diameter of 50 nm and a length of 5 μm, were supplied by Sky Spring Nanomaterials Inc. for this study. The properties of the MWCNTs are specified in Table 2. 

In the first step, PLA is ground using a stone crusher to produce granules with a diameter of 50 µm, which are then further processed into particles. Fine sieves with a diameter of 10 µm are used to separate these particles. In the second step, the PLA is mixed with CNTs in three weight fractions (1%, 3%, and 5%) using a mechanical mixer for one hour. The third step involves using an extruder to produce a homogeneous composite filament with a diameter of 1.75 mm. Figure 1 illustrates the process of composite filament fabrication. In the final step, tensile specimens are printed according to ASTM D638 Type I standards at ±45° angles, with three specimens prepared for each weight fraction. 

In the simulation part of this study, PLA/MWCNT composite brackets with three support designs are developed: a triangular support, an inclined support, and a curved support. Each design is modeled using ANSYS V.11 software.

The observed behavior of PLA/CNT composites in terms of tensile stress and mechanical properties at different printing angles and CNT concentrations can be attributed to several interrelated mechanisms. At low CNT concentrations, the nanomaterials can be uniformly distributed within the PLA matrix. This uniform dispersion enhances interfacial bonding between the PLA and CNTs, allowing for effective load transfer when stress is applied. The presence of well-dispersed CNTs enhances the loadbearing capacity of the composite, improving tensile strength and overall mechanical performance (4, 17, 31). As CNT concentration increases beyond a certain threshold (in this case, 3 wt.%), agglomeration occurs, leading to the formation of clusters. These clusters disrupt the continuity of the PLA matrix. The agglomerated areas can create weak spots within the composite, where stress is concentrated but not effectively transferred (32). This results in a reduction in tensile strength as the composite can no longer efficiently bear the load. When printed in the direction of the load (0°), the CNTs are better aligned with the stress application. This alignment facilitates effective load transfer along the length of the CNTs, enhancing the tensile strength and stiffness of the composite. The mechanical properties (strength, Poisson's ratio, shear modulus) are generally higher in this orientation because the aligned CNTs provide better reinforcement and structural integrity. At a 90° printing angle, the CNTs are not aligned with the stress direction. This misalignment can lead to inefficient load transfer and reduced reinforcement effect, resulting in lower tensile strength and mechanical properties. The misalignment contributes to anisotropic behavior in the mechanical properties, where the material exhibits different strengths depending on the loading direction (33).

When printed in the load direction (0°), the CNTs are better aligned, which enhances load transfer and increases overall tensile strength. The higher alignment and stiffness lead to reduced lateral deformation capabilities, causing the Poisson's ratio to decrease with increasing CNT weight percent (36). The composite becomes less flexible and more rigid in response to axial loading, leading to a lower ratio. The behavior of the composites is highly anisotropic, meaning that mechanical properties can vary significantly based on loading direction. The inherent properties of the PLA matrix play a critical role. At low CNT concentrations, the matrix retains more of its original properties, contributing to a higher Poisson's ratio. As more CNTs are added, the matrix becomes stiffer and more constrained due to the reinforcement, leading to lower Poisson's ratios.

MWCNTs have a high surface area and strong van der Waals interactions between their surfaces. At higher concentrations, these attractive forces can lead to the formation of aggregates or clusters, as the CNTs pull together instead of remaining evenly dispersed within the PLA matrix. Without adequate dispersants or surfactants to stabilize the CNTs, the van der Waals forces can dominate, causing agglomeration. At lower concentrations (such as 1 or 3 wt.%), the CNTs are less likely to agglomerate because the distance between individual CNTs is greater, allowing for better dispersion within the matrix. However, at 5 wt.%, the increased density of CNTs enhances the likelihood of particleparticle interactions, resulting in clustering. The concentration at which CNTs start to form a continuous network within the matrix can lead to increased agglomeration, as there are more opportunities for CNTs to interact with each other rather than remain evenly dispersed (38).

The addition of MWCNTs at 3 wt.% improves the stiffness of the PLA matrix, which leads to reduced deflections under load. This is due to the effective load transfer from the PLA matrix to the well-dispersed MWCNTs, enhancing the overall mechanical performance of the brackets. At 3 wt.%, MWCNTs are likely to be well-dispersed, contributing positively to the mechanical properties without significant agglomeration . As previously discussed, at 5 wt.%, the MWCNTs tend to agglomerate due to van der Waals forces, leading to a decrease in the effective cross-sectional area available for load transfer (2). This aggregation creates weak points in the material, resulting in lower overall stiffness and higher deflections . The presence of agglomerates reduces the effective Young's modulus of the composite. A lower modulus indicates that the material is more compliant (flexible), leading to increased deflections under the same load conditions. The differences in maximum deflections between the 0° and 90° printing angles can be attributed to the alignment of the CNTs with respect to the loading direction. At 0°, CNTs are aligned with the load, which optimizes load transfer, while at 90°, the misalignment reduces the effective reinforcement, leading to higher deflections. The slight variations in SIC values between bracket types (triangular, inclined, and curved) may reflect differences in stress distribution and loadbearing capabilities due to their unique shapes and orientations under load . 

## **4. CONCLUSION** 

This study underscores the critical interplay between CNT concentration and geometric design in optimizing the mechanical characteristics of PLA/MWCNT composites, highlighting how variations in geometry impact stress distribution and overall material performance. The main findings are as follows: 

- The tensile stress of the PLA/CNT composite increases up to 3 wt.% MWCNT due to effective load transfer from the well-dispersed nanomaterials. However, at 5 wt.%, tensile stress decreases due to agglomeration, which disrupts the material's continuity and load-bearing capacity . 

- The mechanical properties, including tensile strength and stiffness, are significantly affected by the printing angle. At a 0° printing angle, where the CNTs are aligned with the load direction, the composite exhibits higher strength and lower deflection compared to a 90° angle, where the misalignment reduces effective load transfer . - The Poisson's ratio varies with CNT concentration and printing angle. A higher Poisson's ratio at 1 wt.% is linked to effective stress distribution and flexibility, while increases in CNT concentration lead to reduced lateral deformation capacity, resulting in lower Poisson's ratios at higher concentrations. 

- Effective dispersion of MWCNTs is crucial for enhancing the mechanical properties of the composite. At 1 wt.%, the CNTs are better dispersed, contributing to improved mechanical performance. At 3 wt.%, the filling of voids by CNTs further enhances tensile stress. In contrast, at 5 wt.%, agglomeration results in reduced tensile strength due to weak points in the material . 

- The geometric configuration of the brackets (triangular, inclined, curved) affects their deflection and stress intensity coefficients. Variations in shape lead to different stress distribution patterns, which influence overall stiffness and mechanical performance under load . - The study highlights the anisotropic behavior of the PLA/CNT composites, where mechanical properties vary significantly based on loading direction. The alignment of CNTs in the printing direction (0°) enhances tensile strength but reduces lateral deformability, while misalignment (90°) leads to higher deflections and lower mechanical performance .
