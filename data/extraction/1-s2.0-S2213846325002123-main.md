## Digital process chain: Scientific based manufacturing of an intake fan blisk prototype 

Seimann, M.[a] *; Kienast, P.[a] ; Rudel, V[a] ; Rohe, P.[a] ; Ganser, P.[a] ; Bergs, T.[a,b]

## **Abstract** 

The manufacturing of prototype components for engine rotors demands the highest standards of the manufacturing processes used, machine tool programming and process design. On the one hand, process chains are often used due to the small number of components required, which differ considerably from series production. On the other hand, the processes used are not optimized for productivity in the context of series production. A special challenge in the process chain is the machining of the airfoil / the flow geometry of the component while carrying out as less iterations as possible for process design, as these are associated with high costs for the required raw parts and tools. According to the current state of the art, machine programming and process design in the manufacturing of blisk prototypes is almost experienced based. This paper presents and describes a digitized process chain that was used to manufacture an intake fan blisk prototype for rig application using no spare part for process design and CAM program validation. It was used just one raw part for machining the final prototype. The content focuses on the scientific methods used throughout the entire manufacturing process development cycle, specially over the process design and machining up to quality assurance.

## **1. Introduction** 

The Covid-19 pandemic had resulted in a 66 % decrease of the worldwide passengers kilometers flown in 2020 [1]. The two largest aircraft manufacturers, AIRBUS and BOEING, expect a full recovery in air traffic between 2023 and 2025 [2,3]. Furthermore, AIRBUS expects a significant increase in the number of aircraft in service worldwide from 22,880 in 2020 to 46,560 in 2042 [2]. 

Most modern commercial aircraft are powered by turbofan engines, in which most of the thrust is generated by the fan of the engine. The fan is normally manufactured in a differential design, which allows the blades and the disk to be manufactured separately and from different materials. The blades and disk are often assembled using profiled slots for connection. The differential design described above reduces the volume of material required compared to production from a solid raw part 

and enables economical production of modern engines with increasing fan diameters. [4–6] 

Since the fan is of enormous importance for the generation of thrust, it is the subject of numerous research and development projects. An investigation of critical operating conditions is essential for the further development of those components. However, these investigations are often not possible in real operation due to different boundary conditions such as economic efficiency and safety. Therefore, downscaled test benches/rigs are often used. [7] 

Compared to the series production of engine rotors, in which several hundred of blisks or disc/blade combinations of the identical design are manufactured per year, the following boundary conditions, among others, must be considered when manufacturing components for test rig operation. On the one hand, only small quantities of the identical design and specially geometry are usually required. Often, only one component is

## **2. State of the art** 

Digital process design and the digital twin in the context of manufacturing are the subject of many research activities. Extensive literature research on the topic can be found in several papers [11,12]. Against the background of the work addressed in this paper, reference is made to paper “Integrating Cloud Computing, Bayesian Optimization, and Neural-Additive Modeling for Enhanced CAM Systems in 5-Axis Milling” [13]. 

Altintas et al. presented an overview [14] in simulating machining processes along the toolpath using virtual environments. Further, they optimize NC programs considering the physical constraints of the machine tool and the cutting process. The main objective is to predict: (i) cutting forces, torque, power; (ii) macro-mechanics: dimensional surface errors; (iii) micro-mechanics: temperature, stresses or strain distributions with high accuracy and within short computation time windows. Cutter workpiece engagement simulations represent the baseline for process simulations. This requires the geometric modelling of the engagement of the cutter at discrete intervals along the toolpath with high resolution settings for tool (within this work CSG-based [15]), toolpath and workpiece (within this work dexel-based [16]). In the context of machining, virtual manufacturing systems support technical employees to design a digital representation of the physical system often tailored for a specific purpose. The range and limitations of digital twins [17] and shadows [18] depend on three primary factors [19]: (i) application: e.g., real-time or offline; (ii) viewpoint: e.g., product, process or systems; (iii) context: e.g., information model and visualization. During the toolpath planning stage, technology models rely on various methods, including analytical, numerical, empirical, or AI based modeling, to predict the physical process conditions [20]. Moreover, there is a growing tendency to integrate these technology models as microservices within a cloud-based framework, with the aim of enhancing the computational capacity of the utilized algorithms [12]. Within a digital twin framework interconnected in the cloud environment [11], these models can interact in a modular and scalable manner. This approach ensures easy accessibility for all stakeholders and facilitates the implementation of data processing services for data-driven process simulations. 

The Fraunhofer IPT has been developing technology models based on dexel-based engagement simulation for more than a decade [21], enabling spatially resolved simulation of various physical effects of the machining process. Fundamental dissertations that consider the cutting force simulation validated the prediction accuracy with a deviation of 15-20 % [22]. The continuous development of the approach led to a strategic development initiative, which was first introduced in the literature under the name ‘dPart®’ [11]. dPart represents a framework for ensuring data-consistent digital twinning in the machining domain. Currently, the technology models of dPart are deployed as microservices within a cloud-based framework to enhance computational efficiency. 

Both [11] and additional publications present case studies based on process simulations that investigate specific technological challenges associated with the machining of thin-walled aerospace components. For instance, [11] investigates static tool deflection during the finishing of a blisk component, while

A key factor in the programming of rotating aeroengine components is the use of modern CAM systems in which the tool paths are planned and calculated based on 3D models of the raw part, the turned geometry and the final part geometry, Figure 2. ## **4. Development of the digital process chain** 

In comparison to the process chain often used in industrial production, in which spare parts are initially manufactured for process design and geometric validation of the flow surfaces, a digital process design was used for the manufacturing of the described blisk/IBR prototype. Thereby, an initial geometric evaluation of the flow surface geometry was carried out based on machine tool internal signals acquired during machining.

The following machining strategies were used for manufacturing planning. The pre-turning of the component up to a final offset of 0.8 mm was carried out in a three-jaw chuck. Furthermore, the milling of the blading was divided into the process steps of pre-roughing, roughing, pre-finishing, and finishing. A pre-roughing operation was used to remove a large amount of material with a milling cutter with indexable inserts. The goal was to avoid distortion caused by residual stresses during machining in the subsequent processes. After roughing, the blade was divided into eleven blocks. The block separation was not carried out equidistantly. The calculation of the blocks and the block steps was based on FEM simulations with the aim of avoiding vibrations and ensure a stiff blade during finishing. The subsequent roughing process was carried out with a torus milling cutter, whereas ball end milling tools were used for semi-finishing and finishing. A total of 132 operations were programmed for the entire machining process using 47 different turning, milling and drilling tools. Figure 3 shows examples of the different turning operations and specific tools used. Figure 4 shows the machining strategies used for blade machining.

In addition to the actual selection and programming of the machining operations in the CAM system, the possibility of material removal simulations and collision control represents an important part of computer-aided manufacturing and engineering. A material removal simulation is a tool to simulate the machined material and thus simulating the time-varying workpiece. The material removal simulation is carried out by the relative movement of the tool to the workpiece along the tool path. This makes it possible to generate in-process workpieces (IPW), which can be used as input variables for further simulation steps such as FE modal analyses to predict IPW dynamics. Furthermore, the material removal simulation enables visual inspection of the material removal for individual operations or the entire operation sequence. The material removal simulation can also be used to partially calculate the remaining offset. 

In addition to the material removal simulation, collision control was also an essential part of the digital manufacturing process chain before the part machining. This served to detect any collisions between the tool, finished part, raw part or IPW 

and the machine tool. Additionally, the rapid motion movement was checked, and extra movements were added to avoid collisions. During the calculation, an iterative change is made to the posted NC code in the program until there were no more collisions.

## _4.3. Process design with coupled technology models_ 

The process design and thus process parameter selection for milling the airfoil in industrial manufacturing is normally based on iterative milling of spare parts. In addition to tool wear, the selection of process parameters focuses on avoiding vibrations during finishing. If, due to the tooth passing frequency of the milling tool, a natural frequency of the block to be produced is hit on the blades or the tool, surface defects occur on the component due to the associated vibrations. In case of the blisk described in this paper, the process design was performed by using a simulation environment (Figure 5). 

Input data (A) such as NC path, raw part geometry, tool geometry, and process parameters are utilized for executing process simulations of coupled technology models (B), such as macro or microscale engagement and cutting force simulations. The engagement simulation serves as a basis for calculating the geometric engagement conditions of cutting process. A multidexel model is preferred, as it offers the best balance between simulation time and accuracy. The force simulation uses a semi-empirical approach to calculate the resulting cutting force impacting the workpiece and tool, derived from chip geometry and empirically determined force coefficients. The resulting output data include the in-process workpiece and cutting

forces, which are further used to calculate additional technological effects such as static tool and workpiece deflection or self-excited so-called chatter vibrations. 

The main objective is to consider physical constraints of the planned machining process by predicting: (i) cutting forces, torque, power; (ii) macro-mechanics: dimensional surface errors; (iii) micro-mechanics: temperature, stresses or strain distributions with high accuracy and within short computation time windows. Through the simulation environment, the need for spare parts can be avoided, which leads to lower costs, cycle time and resources.

## _4.4. Data acquisition (DAQ) and machining_ 

High-performance cutting processes are marked by their high dynamic demands. Achieving the necessary detail of the virtual process representation, the digital shadow [18], requires capturing time-dependent process data (such as axis drive currents, real-time positions, etc.) at a sufficiently high sampling rate. Figure _6_ illustrates the applied spindle load for a pre-roughing operation in block 1 acquired from the SINUMERIK 840D sl machine controller of a Hermle C42 machining center. The programmed full slot cut of the tool is clearly visible in the middle path, where the spindle power reaches the highest amplitudes up to 17.61 Nm. The 3D visualization also clearly shows when the tool is not engaged with the workpiece which is represented by the dark blue color. The following process-related cutting parameters were set: (i) federate = 132 mm/min; (ii) cutting speed = 37 m/min; spindle speed = 294 min[-1] . Monitoring spindle torque in a milling center offers several potential benefits for process optimization and quality control. Real-time torque data provides insight into tool wear, material engagement, and machining stability. Variations in torque can indicate tool deflection, misalignment, or inconsistencies in material properties, allowing for early corrective actions. Monitoring torque enables optimization of cutting parameters to avoid unnecessary energy consumption, as excessive torque may indicate inefficient cutting conditions. 

The electric energy consumption 𝐸 of a spindle motor can be influenced by three main factors, including the spindle load 𝑀; the spindle speed 𝑛 and process time 𝑇 (Figure 7). 𝐸= 2𝜋𝑀𝑛𝑇 (1) 

Spindle load (Figure 7-A): The load on the spindle, or the torque applied to the spindle, can also affect the energy consumption of the motor. If the load is high, the motor will need to work harder and will consume more energy. This was especially the case for roughing operation types of the fan blisk milling processes. By integrating real-time spindle load data into the digital twin framework, adaptive CAM strategies can be developed to balance material removal rates while reducing excessive energy demand. 

Spindle speed (Figure 7-B): As the speed increases, the motor will typically consume more energy. This is because the motor must work harder to rotate the spindle at higher speeds. This was especially the case for the shroud, finishing, hub and blend operation types of the fan blisk milling processes. The digital twin framework can leverage this insight to recommend optimal spindle speeds that minimize power requirements without compromising surface quality. 

Process Time (Figure 7-C): The longer the motor runs, the more energy it will consume. This was especially the case for the roughing, finishing and blend operation types of the fan

## _4.6. CAQ_ 

Quality assurance and documentation of the component geometry was carried using both, CMM and fringe light projection measurement. In general, profile deviations are measured and evaluated at different heights along the stacking axis of the blade profile. The location of the profile sections is clearly defined in the technical drawings. According to the quality requirements, the profile deviations of the blades are limited to 150 µm. Figure 8 illustrates, that both the tool-side and the workpiece-side represent flexible structures, which are highly prone to excessive deflection and self-excited vibrations during the machining process. Key dimensions include in this case: 

- Finishing Tool: 16 mm diameter, 63 mm overhang length, 200 mm holder length. 

- Fan-Blisk: 200 mm profile height, 2.7 mm maximum profile thickness, and 127 mm chord length.

## **5. Dynamic process stability and strategy for chatter-free machining** 

This section presents the procedure used to achieve chatterfree machining for finishing through selection of advantageous spindle speeds individually for each machining block. First, relevant cutter locations were defined. Secondly, the FE model is calibrated to ensure accurate prediction of IPW frequency response functions (FRFs). Finally, the process simulation with coupled technology models including cutter-workpiece engagement, force, FE modal analysis and stability calculation was setup (see Figure 9). 

**==> picture [236 x 123] intentionally omitted <==**

Figure 9: Procedure of chatter-free selection of spindle speeds for finishing 

_Definition of cutter locations and generation of IPWs_ : Based on the programmed toolpath, cutter locations (CLs) were selected as proposed by Rudel and Kienast et al. in [12]. For this fan blisk, five vertical and 33 (three for each finishing operation) horizontal curves, i.e., cutter location layer (CLL), formed the grid on suction and pressure side of the blade and an offset of 2 mm to the blade edges was defined. The dexel-based engagement simulation is executed to extract CL-dependent IPWs (CLi) on the one hand, and to calculate the engagement angles between tool and workpiece on the other hand.

FE modal analysis is utilized to simulate the dynamics of the remaining relevant cutter locations for finishing. After calibration of the FE model, the CLdependent IPW FRFs can be predicted with a high accuracy. Thereby, the location of the eigenfrequencies is most important for a solid selection of advantageous spindle speeds (Figure 11).
